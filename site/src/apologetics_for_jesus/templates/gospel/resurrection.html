The Resurrection

Not a leap of faith, but a claim historians can weigh like any other.

The Minimal Facts Approach

Rather than arguing from the inerrancy of Scripture, many historians — most notably Gary Habermas — build the case for the resurrection from a small set of facts that meet two criteria: they are supported by strong historical evidence, and they are accepted by the overwhelming majority of scholars who study the subject, skeptics included (Habermas and Licona). The question then becomes simple: what best explains these facts?

Fact One: Jesus Died by Crucifixion

Jesus' execution under Pontius Pilate is attested not only by all four Gospels but by non-Christian sources, including the Jewish historian Josephus and the Roman historian Tacitus. Crucifixion was a Roman method of execution designed to be inescapable, and Roman soldiers — whose lives were forfeit if a prisoner survived — had every incentive to confirm death. Essentially no serious historian disputes that Jesus was crucified and died.

Fact Two: The Disciples Had Experiences They Believed Were Appearances of the Risen Jesus

Whatever explanation one gives, virtually all scholars agree the earliest disciples sincerely believed they encountered Jesus alive after His death — individually and in groups, indoors and outdoors, to skeptics and believers alike. Paul preserves a creed, likely formulated within just a few years of the crucifixion, listing a sequence of these appearances:

“and that He appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve. After that He appeared to more than five hundred brethren at one time, most of whom remain until now, but some have fallen asleep;”

1 Corinthians 15:5–6

Fact Three: The Disciples Were Transformed

The men who scattered and hid after Jesus' arrest went on to proclaim His resurrection publicly, at the cost of persecution and, in many accounts, their own lives. People will die for what they sincerely believe is true; they rarely die for what they know to be a lie. This transformation is difficult to explain if the disciples had simply invented the story.

Fact Four: Hostile and Skeptical Witnesses Converted

Two conversions stand out precisely because neither man had reason to be sympathetic. Paul (Saul of Tarsus) actively persecuted the early church before becoming one of its most prominent missionaries, and James, the brother of Jesus, was not a follower during Jesus' ministry (John 7:5) yet became a leader of the Jerusalem church. Both later attributed their conversion to a personal encounter with the risen Jesus (1 Corinthians 9:1, 15:7).

Weighing the Alternatives

Naturalistic explanations — that the disciples hallucinated, that they stole the body, that Jesus merely survived the crucifixion, or that the story developed as legend over time — each account for some of these facts but struggle to explain all of them together without significant strain. Group hallucinations of the kind required are not a recognized psychological phenomenon; a stolen body does not explain the disciples' willingness to die for what they would have known was a fraud; and the early dating of the 1 Corinthians 15 creed leaves little time for legend to displace eyewitness memory (Wright). The resurrection hypothesis, by contrast, accounts for all of the facts at once.
